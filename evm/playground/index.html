A decentralized automatic auction

Can time balance
supply and demand?

A seller offers a price. Demand may arrive now, later, or never. How should that price respond without someone continually adjusting it?

Pulse proposes a rule that lets waiting and buying shape the next price.

Waiting drops. Buying pumps.

For the drop, Pulse uses a Dutch auction: the asking price falls while nobody buys. Its curve approaches a floor, treating the wait as a signal of cooling demand.

For the pump, Pulse uses the Price–Time Scale, or PTS. A purchase lifts the next ask above the price just paid. The time spent waiting sets the target size of that lift:

pump target = PTS × waiting time

Count the wait from the last purchase—or from opening for the first purchase.

A longer wait targets a larger pulse; a shorter wait targets a smaller one. Each purchase starts the next descending curve.

The mathematics

The drop follows a Dutch auction curve. In the continuous model, the ask decays as time t moves away from anchor A. B is the floor; k determines the curve.

p(t) = B + k / (t − A)

The pump is set by PTS. For a positive wait Δ, a purchase accepts price P and sets target pulse R = PTS × Δ. Core V1 chooses P as the next floor. With that choice, the ideal next curve starts at P + R. Here, u is time since that purchase:

pnext(u) = P + k / (u + k/R)

A larger pulse does not necessarily mean a higher absolute next ask: the price paid has also been falling. This is a proposition for price discovery, not a proof of equilibrium.

In the released Core

V1 uses integer seconds and payment units. For a purchase at τ, in an epoch that started at s:

P = B + ⌊k / max(1, τ − A)⌋ R = pts × max(τ − s, 1) d = ⌊k / R⌋ B_next = P; A_next = τ − d p_next(u) = P + ⌊k / max(1, d + u)⌋

The pump calculation uses a one-second minimum wait. Rounding can make the actual lift differ from its target; when R > k, the opening lift is clamped to k.

Implementation note: the floor

Core V1 sets next floor = last sale. This simple ratchet retains each accepted price, aiming to limit eventual supply as further purchases become harder.

The floor rule is one practical way to run Pulse, not a requirement of the drop-and-pump proposition. The resulting supply depends on demand; no fixed final quantity is guaranteed.
