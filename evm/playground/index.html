Can time balance
supply and demand?

A seller offers a price. Demand may arrive now, later, or never. How should that price respond without someone continually adjusting it?

Pulse proposes a rule that lets waiting and buying shape the next price.

Waiting drops. Buying pumps.

For the drop, Pulse uses a Dutch auction: the asking price falls while nobody buys. Its curve approaches a floor, treating the wait as a signal of cooling demand.

For the pump, Pulse uses the Price–Time Scale, or PTS. A purchase lifts the next ask above the price just paid. The time spent waiting sets the target price increase:

target price increase = PTS × waiting time

A longer wait targets a larger pulse; a shorter wait targets a smaller one. Each purchase starts the next descending curve.

The mathematics

The drop follows a Dutch auction curve. In the continuous model, the ask decays as time t moves away from anchor A. B is the floor; k determines the curve.

p(t) = B + k / (t − A)

For a positive waiting time Δ, PTS sets the target price increase R:

R = PTS × Δ

A larger increase does not necessarily mean a higher absolute next ask: the price paid has also been falling. This is a proposition for price discovery, not a proof of equilibrium.

Pulse Core implementation.

Pulse Core V1 is a stateless calculator: it returns prices and the next curve, while application contracts store their own auction state and handle purchases. It implements one practical floor policy:

next floor = previous sale price

PTS sets the target price increase; this floor ratchet sets the baseline. Each accepted sale price becomes the next floor. Waiting can lower the ask toward it, while the floor itself can only rise or stay level.

The rule keeps the implementation simple and aims to constrain further issuance through price. It guarantees neither a fixed supply nor an eventual stop. It is a choice made by Core V1, separate from the broader proposition that waiting drops and buying pumps.

Timing and exact calculations

Core V1 counts waiting time from the last purchase—or from opening for the first purchase. The price-increase calculation uses a one-second minimum wait.

With sale price P as the next floor and target increase R, the ideal next curve starts at P + R. Here, u is time since the purchase:

pnext(u) = P + k / (u + k/R)

V1 uses integer seconds and payment units. For a purchase at τ, in an epoch that started at s:

P = B + ⌊k / max(1, τ − A)⌋ R = pts × max(τ − s, 1) d = ⌊k / R⌋ B_next = P; A_next = τ − d p_next(u) = P + ⌊k / max(1, d + u)⌋

When R > k, the actual price increase is capped at k.

Pulse is a DAA.

Decentralized Automatic Auction. The commitment is to make the rules public and fix them before launch. Time and purchases move the price; neither an operator nor an adaptive mechanism rewrites the rules to steer the outcome.

That means being open to both success and failure. Demand may grow, settle, or fade. Whatever the response, let it stand as evidence of demand under the chosen rules.

Each project must enforce this commitment in its own auction contract. Using Pulse Core alone does not make an application immutable.

Where Pulse fits.

Pulse suits experiments whose outcomes are shaped by the people who take part.

Experimental art. For an artwork released in editions, buying, waiting, and the resulting price history can become part of the work itself.

Studying price and participation. Study Pulse itself through simulations or separate auctions with different fixed settings. Does a larger PTS change how long people wait? How does the rising floor affect further sales?

Pulse fits projects ready to welcome success, accept failure, and learn from either.
